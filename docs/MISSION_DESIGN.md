# Proxima mission: design baseline for discussion

Status: conceptual study, not a feasible or certified spacecraft design.
Numbers marked as sensitivity cases are not hardware predictions.

## Current selected revision

32 people, 16 shared cabins, 20 years onboard (19.9 transfer plus 0.1 arrival),
1,000 kg edible spare food. Two 19.5 m-radius three-deck discs remain.
A compact 10 t complete-diet food-factory target and 60 m2 garden replace
large carried crop modules. Large surface greenhouses are built locally.
The factory operates during burns and coast; reserve endurance is about 51 days.
The separate 1 t process inventory is not edible emergency food.

Selected staged planning masses: 512.3 t all mission hardware with 25% allowance,
342.1 t surface hardware with allowance, 386.4 t after touchdown, 1,330.8 t departure,
382.5 t propulsion antimatter plus equal matter and 8.86 t touchdown hydrogen.
About 182.7 t flight hardware, margin and unused reserve stays off the lander.
The 31 t dust shield, main cruise engine, bulk storage, spin bearings, standoff
frame and excess hot panels separate before independent descent. A dedicated
10 t descent engine, 2 t local store, 2 t separation allowance and required hot
cooling are explicit unvalidated targets. All flight mass still reaches Proxima.
The 12 km/s direct arrival allowance splits 2 km/s pre-separation / 10 km/s descent;
orbit acquisition, separation and disposal remain unsolved. The 0.400 km/s thermal
budget assumes a conditional 1 km switch with unqualified plume/radiation suitability.
Peak speed is 0.2104c; each main burn is 62.99 onboard days.
The 0.5c whole-engine exhaust and complete food plant remain unvalidated targets.
A new unselected engine-reuse comparison keeps the cruise engine through direct
descent and releases it at hydrogen handover. With unchanged conservative thermal
hardware allowances: 376.3 t settled, 8.62 t hydrogen; stage separation/divert remains
unresolved. See [engine reuse comparison](ENGINE_REUSE_OPTIONS.md).
See [current mass and fuel basis](MASS_BUDGET.md) and
[current habitat programme](HABITAT_INTERIOR_DESIGN.md) for authoritative figures.

The detailed sections following the agreed requirements record earlier design
iterations and comparison studies. Their 36-person, 16-year, larger-disc, stored-food
or large carried-farm figures are historical and superseded by this revision.

## Agreed requirements

- 32 colonists: 16 men and 16 women, early twenties at departure, in 16 couples.
- Each couple shares a cabin with two convertible ordinary/torpor beds.
- Partners wake together: seven days awake, 21 days in torpor during cruise.
- At least three fit crew on duty at all times; recovery must be accounted for.
- 20 years aboard to arrival; target 1.2g powered flight.
- Brake at the destination; no high-speed flyby.
- Antimatter-powered interstellar propulsion, explicit storage and cooling.
- Dust, gas, cosmic-radiation and engine-radiation protection.
- Multi-deck rotating discs, edge-on to flight, with gravity decreasing inward.
- Cruise gravity target: 0.5 Earth g at the outer cabin floors.
- 500 tonnes departure wet mass is an optimisation target, not a hard limit.
- Carry the settlement equipment on the same ship.
- Bring both habitat discs, all flight cabins, and as much useful service
  infrastructure as practical to the surface for continued occupation.
- Staged settlement delivery: flight-only hardware stays off the lander. Surface
  hardware never flies again; no settlement ascent/future interplanetary reserve.
- Landing acceleration is driven by braking needs and actual crew, structural
  and equipment limits, rather than capped at the 1.2g interstellar setting.
- Remote exploration package strictly below 500 kg all-in, excluding crewed
  rovers. Carry small scouts, microlanders and robotic surface explorers where
  feasible; investigate shared antimatter propulsion support.
- Provide at least one pressurised crewed surface utility rover, separately
  budgeted, reusing a cruise service module and suitable ship equipment.
- Crewed rover endurance: two people for up to seven days; range remains unsized.
- Assume airless Proxima b for design; this is a scenario assumption, not an observation.
- Primary surface electricity from antimatter; emergency solar panels and stored
  energy for critical loads. Separate surface-conversion hardware must be sized.
- Large inflatable greenhouse using processed local regolith as the mineral
  growing substrate, with imported/recycled nutrients and biological inputs.
- Use vertical farming and artificial lighting, with crop-specific CO2 enrichment,
  where these reduce footprint without compromising diet, maintenance or reliability.
- Assume complete production and repair from sufficient accessible local
  materials using advanced fabrication, including repair/reproduction of the
  fabrication equipment. This is an explicit future-technology design premise.
- Carry surface-power antimatter sufficient for 20 years after landing,
  separate from propulsion, transit and landing-contingency inventories.
- Fix surface-power antimatter inventory at 1 kg, plus 1 kg matching ordinary
  matter. This is fuel mass only; containment, conversion and cooling count separately.
- Recover useful waste heat through a temperature-matched, isolated heat network
  before final rejection; include compatible process heating and thermal storage.
- Target first births around nine months after landing; provide maternity and
  neonatal capability before arrival. This is not a guaranteed medical outcome.
- Embryo bank: two couple-related embryos per couple (36 total), plus unrelated
  donor embryos for genetic diversity; donor-bank quantity remains to be sized.
- Habitats use lightweight deployable structures and compact for descent;
  they are not fixed rigid discs.
- Inflatable compartments provide the principal habitable volume in both discs
  and other suitable landed facilities. Use rigid construction only where loads,
  equipment, containment or occupied-descent requirements justify it.
- Investigate direct antimatter thrust for high-altitude orbital braking and
  antimatter-heated hydrogen for terminal descent. Destination Proxima b.
- Prefer continuous direct-antimatter braking as low as spacecraft exposure,
  plume/ground disturbance and post-landing access allow, followed by a short
  hydrogen-heated touchdown burn. No fixed 100 km switch is imposed.
- In the genuinely barren/unoccupied airless case, prompt irradiation of ground
  is acceptable in principle; evaluate damage, activation and vehicle effects
  rather than treating human dose to unoccupied ground as a prohibition.

Current preference: the entire spacecraft lands as one integrated vehicle, with
its inflatable habitats compacted but attached. Their cabins, frames and service
hardware redeploy after touchdown. No replacement surface cabins are carried.
Engine operation near the surface, whole-ship landing loads and attitude are
unresolved feasibility gates. The orbital-tug / separate nuclear-thermal descent
architecture below remains a comparison case, not the current preferred plan.
Landing site remains unspecified. Use an airless environment as the agreed
design scenario while retaining conditional mass/radius and surface composition.

## Flight architecture and surface reuse

During interstellar flight, a stationary structure carries two longitudinally
separated, counter-rotating discs with transverse axles. Their planes contain
the flight direction. Align their frontal silhouettes to share a dust shield.
Supporting structure must clear the rotating volumes; a stationary longitudinal
spine cannot pass through their occupied rotating interiors.

Each deployable disc contains nine double cabins, independent isolatable pressure zones,
concentric flight decks, and local life-support distribution. Both discs must
support all 36 people awake collectively. Split clinical, food and essential
life-support capabilities so neither disc has a sole mission-critical monopoly.
Detailed compartmentation and survival capacity remain to be designed.

Reusable service modules mount on the stationary ship structure during flight.
Provide landing-load paths, lifting points and surface connection ports from the
beginning. Modular equipment can be moved after touchdown; release systems for
independent flight are only required by the separable comparison architecture.
Such interfaces add mass: surface reuse is not free.

The following table applies only to the historical SEPARABLE comparison architecture.
Its future-flight reserves and reusable-tug requirements are not current requirements. In the
preferred integrated study, all listed hardware lands; retained-orbit provisions
are replaced by surface support and any separately required future-flight systems.

| Subsystem | Surface destination in comparison case | Orbital provision in comparison case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Both complete deployable discs and all cabins | Compact, land, redeploy and reconnect | None permanently required |
| Main medical and embryo facilities | Land after a viable receiving habitat exists | Independent transit protection during transfer |
| Main life support and water processing | Land with modular service sections | Small maintenance/visitor system only if required |
| Workshops, robots, food and settlement cargo | Land | Retain minimum tug-service tools and inspection robots |
| Main settlement power | Dedicated landable units | Independent containment/tug power retained |

Do not reuse a pressure tank for potable water without material compatibility
and contamination assessment. Do not count every kilogram of space shielding
as useful surface shielding: landing that mass may cost more than excavating
local material. Preserve required habitat protection until local protection is
actually in place.

## Lightweight deployable habitat construction

Use a hybrid architecture, not a single thin fabric pancake pressure vessel:

- Folding carbon-fibre composite frame members and deployable tension members
  carry rotational/deck loads; positive mechanical locks carry occupied loads.
- Separately isolatable expandable pressure compartments collectively fill the
  disc, including inner decks. Use redundant gas barriers and high-strength
  textile restraint layers (Vectran/aramid candidates), with foldable protective
  and insulating layers. This is still a multi-deck disc, not an occupied rim.
- Use lightweight sandwich floor panels and folding partitions; cabin privacy
  partitions need not all be pressure bulkheads.
- Retain rigid hubs, hatches, medical/bed equipment racks and utility interfaces.
  Use titanium or aluminium locally where bearing, joint or thermal loads justify
  them. Material selection must include fire behaviour, repairability and life.
- Surface foundations support the redeployed decks. Flight suspension members
  alone cannot support the same structure under uniform planetary gravity.

The pressure vessels themselves are inflatable. Use rounded/lobed compartments
supported within a disc-shaped deployable frame; any outer disc cover is not
automatically another pressurised shell. Do not inflate the entire geometric
disc volume merely to obtain a circular appearance. Floor decks occupy several
radii, with enclosed connecting passages; unoccupied bays can remain unpressurised.
The assembly has occupied inner decks and remains a disc, not a ring habitat.
Small compartment curvature helps control membrane loads compared with one vast
flat-sided pressure bag. Doors and junctions still introduce substantial loads.

## Other inflatable landed facilities and surface protection

Use inflatable occupied shells for dining/recreation extensions, clean workshop
space, suitable medical-room extensions, hydroponic enclosures and connecting
passages where their environmental requirements permit. Carry future-growth
shells packed, with their equipment mass separately accounted for; do not count
an empty shell as a functioning greenhouse or clinic. Reuse flight facilities
first and add surface-only volume where it has a specific commissioning purpose.

Keep machinery, cryobank containers, high-temperature work cells, pressure-gas
cylinders, nuclear reactors, cryogenic hydrogen tanks, bearings and landing
load paths in appropriately engineered rigid hardware. A lightweight inflatable
room can surround machinery without replacing its casing or structural mount.

After landing, support occupied floors with deployable foundations and protect
softgoods with abrasion/weather layers. Investigate excavated local material
as shielding on independent arches or berms; do not pile it directly onto an
unqualified membrane. Earth-like soil, accessible water and benign atmospheric
conditions are not assumed. Shielding adequacy requires a dose calculation.
Retain carried protection until local protection is commissioned.

Surface layout should prioritise useful horizontal rooms rather than reproducing
cruise gravity geometry. The same pressure compartments can deploy onto the
redeployed disc frames with reconfigured floors, or connect around their hubs as
low-profile rooms if qualification supports the changed loads. Selection between
those deployment patterns is open; both must reuse all cabin compartments and
the complete disc assemblies. Foundations and frame reuse must be shown rather
than treating the frame as discarded packaging.

NASA's expandable-habitat work supports the layered softgoods approach, not this
specific rotating, repeatedly packed disc. Sixteen years of creep, radiation,
abrasion and thermal ageing followed by deflation/repacking/redeployment need
qualification. Carbon composites and thin membranes do not replace radiation
shielding. Water inventories and removable shielding require their own transfer
and stowage plans. Do not claim an unverified packing ratio or mass saving.

Compaction sequence: stop rotation; secure equipment in rigid transport racks;
evacuate occupants to rigid descent/refuge compartments; recover atmosphere into
storage (storage tanks and compressors included); deflate; fold decks and frame;
enclose the bundle in a protective descent pallet. Bring the complete assembly
down and reverse the process on prepared foundations. Beds and cabin equipment
stay with their assigned habitat but are not occupied during compaction.

In the separable comparison case, each descent/refuge compartment houses 18 occupants.
The integrated case may instead use one rigid refuge for all 36. Either must support them
through deployment delays. It can subsequently serve as an airlock, emergency
refuge or service module. Its mass is additional and must be budgeted. Medical
and embryo stores remain in suitable continuously supported rigid compartments.

Compaction reduces envelope size and may reduce entry/landing hardware size; it
does not remove payload mass or the velocity that propulsion must cancel.

## Gravity and compacted-disc landing

During cruise, down points radially outward. During 1.2g burns the discs should
stop and lock; down then follows thrust gravity. Braking reverses that direction.
During surface operation, down follows planetary gravity.

Study redeploying each disc flat, with its axle vertical. Ground floors then lie
parallel to the broad faces, whereas cruise decks are concentric cylinders.
The same fixed floor layout cannot serve both. Cabins require a deliberately
reconfigurable interior: folding/repositioned floor panels, beds and furnishings,
reoriented fixtures, and access routes that work in each mode. Retain each
couple's cabin pressure compartment and equipment; do not assume a furnished cabin can
rotate through 90 degrees inside an arbitrarily thin disc.

Rotating whole rooms is an alternative, with substantial clearance, bearing and
utility complexity. Prefer examining deployable interior fittings first.
Wet services, toilets, medical access, fire escape routes and equipment restraints
must be checked in all three gravity configurations before selecting a layout.

The cruise gravity target is now 0.5 Earth g at the outer cabin floors. In the
30 m radius study, a 28 m floor requires approximately 4.00 rpm. At the same
angular speed the 24, 20 and 16 m floors have 0.429g, 0.357g and 0.286g.
Place private cabins and clinical functions on the higher-gravity outer decks.
Disc dimensions remain study values; long-term adequacy of 0.5g is not established,
and torpor does not establish immunity to low-gravity effects. Recovery for the
conditional 1.19g surface environment needs to be included in arrival operations.

Projected disc area edge-on is approximately diameter times thickness, not
diameter alone. Compartment pressure shapes need adequate restraint and
structural clearance. Full discs enclose much more volume than rings and do not
automatically weigh less.

## Proxima b: observed properties and conditional design values

The 2025 NIRPS/HARPS study (Suarez Mascareno et al., A&A 700, A11) reports
minimum mass 1.055 +/- 0.055 Earth masses and period 11.18465 +/- 0.00053 days.
It estimates true mass 1.44 +/- 0.21 Earth masses conditional on system
coplanarity and previously inferred inclination. That conditional estimate is
not a direct true-mass measurement. Orbital distance is about 0.0485 AU.

Radius, surface gravity, atmosphere and surface conditions are not measured well
enough to specify a landing vehicle. Adopt 1.44 Earth masses and ASSUME 1.10 Earth
radii for a rocky reference case; this radius is not an observed estimate with
an error bar. The calculator derives about 1.19g surface gravity, 8.9 km/s circular
speed at an assumed 200 km altitude and 12.8 km/s surface escape speed. A 200 km
parking orbit is only a geometric reference until atmospheric extent is known.

Use 9, 10 and 12 km/s descent delta-v sensitivity cases with zero atmospheric
braking credit. Nominal 10 km/s is an initial allowance around the orbital speed,
not an integrated descent result; gravity losses, descent geometry, thrust,
reserves and the actual planet may change it. If an atmosphere exists, entry
heating and drag must be analysed even when taking no braking credit: a vacuum
calculation is not a vehicle design that works in every atmosphere. Do not call
these three cases observational confidence bounds.

The agreed airless scenario is not a claim that an atmosphere has been excluded
observationally. No breathable air, temperate landing zone, accessible ice, magnetic protection
or synchronous rotation is assumed as established fact. Settlement sites and
resource extraction need reconnaissance. Surface structure must support the
conditional local gravity and any atmospheric pressure/wind loads.

## Preferred landing study: direct braking plus thermal touchdown

Use a continuous braking trajectory where feasible: direct antimatter thrust
removes most approach/orbital velocity, then switches to antimatter-heated hydrogen
without an intentional intermediate stop or hover for the remaining approach,
gravity losses, hazard avoidance and touchdown. The whole spacecraft remains
attached and its inflatable habitats remain compacted. This is the current
preferred candidate, not a demonstrated radiation-compatible trajectory.

At 1800 s Isp, thermal delta-v budgets of 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 km/s consume
respectively about 2.9%, 5.8%, 12.0%, 18.5% and 32.7% of final landed mass as
hydrogen. Budgets must include gravity losses and reserves. These are conditional
mass calculations, not predictions of which terminal budget is sufficient.

Stopping high above the surface does not eliminate the energy gained while
falling. As a transparent example, assume an airless vertical descent starting
at rest at the direct-engine cutoff, constant local gravity 1.19g, then coasting
and braking with constant proper acceleration 1.5 times local gravity. For that
simple trajectory delta-v = sqrt(6*g*h). From 100 km this is about 2.65 km/s,
requiring about 16.2% hydrogen by landed mass; from 10 km it is about 0.84 km/s,
requiring about 4.9%. These omit reserves and are not an actual orbital descent.
Their low cutoff altitudes have NOT been shown compatible with direct-exhaust
radiation. They illustrate why choosing a percentage split of a 10 km/s budget
does not define a physically workable landing.

Determine the switch from coupled trajectory AND radiation/thermal analysis:
maximum direct-engine power, source spectrum, crew dose, ground/atmosphere
secondary radiation, exhaust direction and surface heat deposition. Altitude
alone does not make operation acceptable: penetrating radiation can still reach
the planet. Include engine transition transients and retain enough thermal fuel
to complete landing if the direct engine must shut down earlier than nominal.
If radiation forces a distant cutoff, gravity can restore a substantial thermal
propellant requirement. Atmospheric braking remains conditional on actual data.

For a first sensitivity range, carry 1-3 km/s terminal budgets (about 6-19%
hydrogen relative to landed mass) without calling them an accepted reserve or
a safe trajectory. Full-descent thermal and direct modes remain comparisons.
This earlier 1-3 km/s range is a comparison, not a minimum allocation: the current
preference is to minimise the thermal segment and retain only the delta-v and
reserves justified by the continuous approach and transition constraints.

## First solved terminal descent: variable gravity

These rest/coast/brake cases are diagnostic comparisons, NOT the proposed
arrival sequence. The preferred trajectory now brakes continuously through the
mode transition and reaches zero surface-relative speed only at touchdown.

mission_calculations.py now solves an airless radial descent with inverse-square
planetary gravity. This terminal problem begins at rest relative to a nonrotating
planet at a prescribed direct-engine cutoff height. It does NOT solve how the
spacecraft removes orbital velocity to reach that state. Atmospheric drag,
planetary rotation, terrain and source-radiation transport are omitted.

The vehicle coasts, then applies constant proper acceleration to touch down at
zero speed. Thrust is throttled with falling mass to maintain that acceleration.
With surface thrust/weight 1.5, the conditional reference planet implies about
1.79 Earth g of proper acceleration during the burn. This exceeds the 1.2g
interstellar trajectory setting; it is a study case, not approved crew tolerance
or demonstrated landing-engine performance.

| Cutoff height, starting at rest | Thermal delta-v | Hydrogen / final landed mass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 25 km | 1.32 km/s | 7.8% |
| 50 km | 1.86 km/s | 11.1% |
| 100 km | 2.62 km/s | 16.0% |
| 250 km | 4.08 km/s | 26.0% |
| 500 km | 5.64 km/s | 37.7% |
| 1000 km | 7.64 km/s | 54.2% |

All rows use the unproven 1800 s Isp target and omit hover/abort/hazard-avoidance
reserves. Do not treat the cutoff heights as approved for direct antimatter
operation or assume the hypothetical initial rest condition is free to obtain.
Even these propellant values do not include additional engine/tank/shield mass.

The 100 km case coasts for 77.6 s, ignites at 65.7 km while falling at 0.885 km/s,
and burns for 149.5 s. At 2.0 surface thrust/weight (2.38 Earth g) it instead
needs about 12.9% hydrogen; at 3.0 (3.58 Earth g), about 11.1%. Higher thrust
reduces gravity losses but raises engine, structure and occupant load demands.
Do not choose a stronger engine from propellant savings alone.

Verification: the energy condition sets ignition height analytically, with
numerical quadrature for elapsed burn/coast time. An independent forward RK4
integration of the 100 km / 1.5-thrust-weight case reached touchdown within 0.1 m
and 0.001 m/s. Doubling quadrature resolution changes delta-v by less than
1e-8 km/s. Exported 18 height/thrust cases are in descent_sensitivity.csv.
This verifies the stated model, not the actual planet or a flight vehicle.

Radiation geometry is a separate constraint: at 100 km the planet subtends about
41.6% of the sky; at 1000 km, about 25.8%. Those are geometric benchmarks for an
isotropic source, NOT engine emission fractions or doses. A directed particle
nozzle does not establish a harmless gamma field. The next feasibility gate is
an engine-source and radiation-transport specification that constrains where
direct braking may operate, followed by a complete orbital-to-terminal profile.
If the allowable cutoff is distant, a small terminal hydrogen fraction may fail.

## Continuous-braking example and prompt ground-radiation benchmark

There is no airless-world requirement to switch at 100 km. That height was an
arbitrary diagnostic boundary, not an atmospheric interface, safe-radiation
distance or selected mission constraint. The orbital approach, thrust pointing
and engine/radiation constraints must determine the transition state.

The calculator includes a continuous radial segment beginning at 100 km with
downward speed 1.096 km/s. Constant proper acceleration 1.79 Earth g brakes the
ship to rest at the ground in 184 s without coasting or hovering. This is an
illustrative terminal segment, NOT a complete orbit-to-surface trajectory. A
smooth change of propulsion mode is assumed possible without changing thrust.

| Direct-to-thermal switch height | Speed at switch | Remaining hydrogen / landed mass |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 km | 108 m/s | 1.85% |
| 5 km | 242 m/s | 4.19% |
| 10 km | 342 m/s | 5.98% |
| 25 km | 542 m/s | 9.61% |

These use 1800 s thermal Isp, no atmosphere and no operational reserves. Low
switch altitudes are kinematic examples only. They are not approved for direct
engine radiation or plume effects. Acceleration exceeds the 1.2g interstellar
setting; frame, engine and crew requirements must be checked independently.

To quantify the radiation issue, use a separate unshielded benchmark: 1000 t
instantaneous ship mass, 1.2 Earth g proper acceleration, whole-engine u_eff=0.5c,
and isotropic prompt gamma power equal to 38% of annihilation power. The 38%
approximates NASA's 710/1880 MeV prompt neutral-pion energy split; actual engine
anisotropy, shielding and spectrum are not represented. This gives 7.06 PW source
power and 2.68 PW prompt gamma power. For an unobstructed point at source distance:

| Source distance | Instantaneous prompt gamma energy flux |
| --- | --- |
| 1 km | 213 MW/m2 |
| 10 km | 2.13 MW/m2 |
| 100 km | 21.3 kW/m2 |
| 1000 km | 213 W/m2 |

Scale linearly with thrust and gamma escape fraction, inversely with distance
squared. These are photon energy fluxes, not burn-integrated doses or ordinary
visible thermal illumination. The continuous-descent example has higher proper
acceleration and would radiate more at the same instantaneous mass.

For a dose SCALE ONLY, applying the NIST 10 MeV water mass energy-absorption
coefficient 0.01566 cm2/g (0.001566 m2/kg) gives a water dose proxy about 33 Gy/s
at 100 km in this benchmark. The annihilation spectrum is not monoenergetic
10 MeV; this is not a human-organ dose prediction. Charged-particle equilibrium,
secondary transport, shielding and actual spectral energy deposition require
Monte Carlo analysis. Do not infer a safe exposure distance from this proxy.

Prompt irradiation ends after shutdown; the ground is not automatically a
permanent radioactive waste site. However photonuclear/hadronic reactions can
activate material, and the magnitude/lifetime depend on spectrum and soil.
An airless, uninhabited site might tolerate some irradiation, but the spacecraft
must tolerate backscatter, secondary particles, soil ejecta and any subsequent
surface exposure. Directed particle exhaust striking the ground is omitted
from the table and can add substantial energy, excavation and secondary radiation.

Next step: solve the full moving approach and source terms rather than impose
an arbitrary cutoff. Consider shallow approaches that keep the particle beam
clear of the surface while direct thrust is used, then transition to thermal
thrust. Such pointing cannot remove the undirected prompt gamma field. No fixed
switch altitude or acceptable ground-damage level has yet been selected.

Design priority for a barren/unoccupied site: minimise touchdown hydrogen subject
to crew/electronics/containment exposure, acceptable excavation/ejecta, vehicle
loads and a usable post-landing site. Prompt irradiation of empty ground is not
itself an exclusion criterion. Do not assume this establishes that Proxima b is
actually airless or lifeless. A short thermal burn is selected; its altitude,
duration, fuel reserve and engine mass are not yet established.

## Shared antimatter-heated hydrogen installation

Keep both compacted inflatable discs and useful service infrastructure attached
through descent. Use a high-thrust antimatter-heated hydrogen mode: meter
annihilation energy into hydrogen through an engineered absorber/heat-transfer
system and exhaust through thermal nozzles. Share the antimatter inventory,
containment support and metering infrastructure where feasible; a separate
thermal reaction chamber and nozzle installation may still be necessary.

Retain 1800 s vacuum Isp as an unproven sizing target, with 1500-2000 s sensitivity.
It is not guaranteed by replacing a fission heat source with antimatter. At
the previous full-thermal 10 km/s comparison, hydrogen expenditure is ideally 76% of final landed
mass. The 1000 t landed sensitivity vehicle needs about 762 t hydrogen before
landing: it starts descent near 1762 t, before distinct service consumption or
unmodelled reserves. Final landed mass includes all engine and tank dry hardware.

The conceptual flow is: independent hydrogen tanks -> pumps -> regenerative
cooling/heat-transfer channels -> thermal chamber -> gimballed nozzles. A separate
metered antimatter feed supplies the energy-release region. The physical absorber,
working-fluid interaction, shielding and energy-capture efficiency remain to be
designed; no assumed gamma-ray mirror is used.

This may avoid a separate fission reactor, but adds absorption, shielding and
heat rejection. Sharing fuel storage does not establish a lighter installation.
At 1 MN thrust, exhaust kinetic power alone is about 8.8 GW at the 1800 s target.
Radiative leakage and extra heat increase required source power. The existing
interstellar nozzle is not assumed capable of thermal landing operation.

Full direct beamed-core landing is only a comparison; the selected hybrid study
uses it for early braking, not assumed surface operation. Thermal touchdown
retains its own propellant and hardware requirements.

The whole vehicle must land, including propulsion machinery, containment, radiators
and landing contingencies. Space scouts deploy beforehand; no main-ship future
flight reserve is required. Landing extra propulsion dry mass can outweigh reactor savings.
The integrated mass calculation must replace, not silently reuse, an orbital-tug
mass case. Near-surface engine operation remains unproven in both modes.

Use about 1.5 local thrust/weight as a preliminary sizing point, not an engine-out
or trajectory guarantee. In the conditional 1.19g planetary case, thrust giving
only 1.2g proper acceleration provides almost no hover margin. Interstellar burn
acceleration is a trajectory setting, not a demonstrated engine thrust ceiling;
maximum engine thrust at landing mass must be calculated independently.

Whole-vehicle geometry is not selected. A vertical engine-down landing needs
column strength, a sufficiently wide support footprint and a feasible surface
deployment orientation for both discs. A horizontal landing needs downward thrust
from reoriented engines or additional nozzles plus beam-like landing load paths.
Do not simply draw a horizontal spacecraft hovering on an axial engine. Folding
long trusses/radiators while maintaining containment, heat rejection and thrust
load paths must be included. Enclosing tanks and equipment for atmospheric entry
can negate softgoods packing savings. No atmosphere is assumed available to help.

No main-ship ascent or future-flight fuel reserve is carried. Landing contingencies
and the separate robotic exploration fuel inventory remain explicit requirements.

## Comparison case: advanced nuclear-thermal descent

Study a closed-cycle gas-core nuclear-thermal engine (nuclear-lightbulb family),
heating hydrogen while attempting to retain the fissioning fuel. Use 1800 s
vacuum Isp as an unproven design target, with 1500-2000 s sensitivity and a 900 s
solid-core fallback comparison. Closed-cycle fuel retention does not eliminate
neutron/gamma radiation, shielding, accident consequences or exhaust hazards.
No flight-ready engine meeting this landing specification exists.

At nominal 10 km/s, ideal initial/landed mass ratios are 1.97 (1500 s), 1.76
(1800 s), and 1.67 (2000 s), versus 9.64 at 450 s. For every 100 t landed the
1800 s case needs about 76 t descent propellant, excluding separate service
consumption; landed mass already includes engines, tanks, shielding, refuge,
landing supports and residual fuel. It is not 100 t of habitat plus free engines.
Engine mass, sufficient thrust, cooling and tank mass can offset savings.

Use local TWR 1.5 as a preliminary engine-sizing point, not a solved trajectory:
about 17.5 kN thrust per starting tonne in the conditional reference gravity.
Design multiple engines, vectoring, balance, shutdown/throttle control and
engine-out behaviour before stating redundancy. Distributed nozzles need plume
clearance from compacted structures and ground debris; geometry is unresolved.

Hydrogen needs long-duration storage with explicit refrigeration power and
boil-off accounting. Landing tanks and their supports do not compact like the
habitat. Investigate reusing drained tanks on the surface only if suitable.
Reusing descent reactors for settlement power would require deliberately
dual-mode machinery, electrical conversion, shielding and cooling; it is not
automatic. Keep independent landable power in the baseline until assessed.

## Trajectory and crew arithmetic

At 1.2g, a symmetric 16-year proper-time transfer over 4.2465 light-years has
approximately 78.4 days in each burn, 15.571 years coasting, and peak speed 0.260c.
It lasts approximately 16.56 years in the departure rest frame. This ignores
relative stellar motion, local orbital manoeuvres, navigation reserves and staging.

Stagger the 18 couples' cruise cycles evenly over 28 days. A new couple begins
its awake period every 37 hours 20 minutes. There are eight or ten awake people,
averaging nine. A possible nominal duty pattern is two 12-hour watches with four
people each at minimum population. Three on duty plus one relief is only a
numerical roster; post-torpor recovery can remove that margin. Continuous
qualified staffing still requires a recovery and skills schedule.

Continuous cycling gives four awake years in sixteen. If everyone is awake for
both burns, the 16-year transfer gives 4.322 awake years and 11.678 torpor years,
roughly 203 cruise cycles per person. This is an exception to 7/21 over the whole
mission and needs a design decision. Do not assume torpor stops ageing: a crew
departing aged 20-24 arrives chronologically aged 36-40. Repeated 21-day torpor
with seven-day awake periods is not established safe human medicine.

## Propulsion and mass feasibility

Use u_eff = thrust / ship mass-loss rate, including energy lost in undirected
radiation and all exhaust species. Do not substitute a charged pion's speed for
the whole engine's effective exhaust velocity.

For rapidity eta = atanh(v/c), ideal retained/departure mass accounting gives:

    departure / retained = exp(2 * eta / (u_eff/c))

At the 16-year trajectory this is 5.886 at 0.3c and 2.897 at 0.5c. Thus:

| Retained mass at arrival | Departure at 0.3c | Departure at 0.5c |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 200 t | 1,177 t | 579 t |
| 400 t | 2,354 t | 1,159 t |
| 600 t | 3,532 t | 1,738 t |

These are sensitivity cases, not estimates of the ship's component masses.
Retained mass includes descent vehicles and their propellant, surface equipment,
remaining consumables, and any robotic exploration package not yet deployed.
Historical orbital-tug cases included future propulsion fuel; that is no longer
a main-ship requirement. Transit food
and wastes require a time-dependent mass history; conserving waste aboard is
different from jettisoning it. These constant-u cases omit separate service losses.

For pure equal-mass matter/antimatter annihilation with no added reaction mass,
half of the expended mass is antimatter. The 400 t, 0.5c example consumes roughly
379 t of antimatter. Antimatter-catalysed fusion has different fuel accounting
and cannot inherit the same exhaust performance without an engine model.

At 1.2g that example initially requires roughly 13.6 MN thrust and 8.2 PW of
annihilation power under the pure-annihilation assumptions. Absorbing even one
millionth of that power leaves about 8.2 GW to reject. At 1500 K and emissivity
0.9, this needs approximately 32,000 square metres of emitting area, before
thermal gradients, plumbing, blockage and hardware limits. Two radiating faces
can reduce panel planform, not emitting area. Cold habitat/cryogenic circuits
need separate, much colder heat rejection and refrigeration input power.

No hardware has demonstrated the required fuel storage, engine performance or
combined thermal performance. Increasing ship mass cannot by itself fix this:
at constant acceleration, thrust and power rise with mass.

Keep landing contingency fuel and robotic exploration propellant as separate
lines. No reusable orbital tug or main-ship future-flight reserve is required.
Exploration carriers need independent power, containment, thermal control,
navigation and communications after deployment; none of that is free payload.

### Antimatter electricity with emergency solar

Use a purpose-designed low-power annihilation/conversion installation, sharing
suitable storage, metering and support hardware from flight. Do not run the open
interstellar exhaust nozzle as a power station. Containment, shielding, thermal
conversion, generators and radiators must be sized for continuous surface use.
Preserve a dedicated surface-power inventory after landing; removal of future
spaceflight fuel does not remove the need for this surface reserve.

Solar backup includes its own controls, stored energy, starting capability and
essential containment/life-support loads. Panel area, illumination, spectrum,
temperature and dust determine output; panels alone do not supply power in
darkness. Emergency backup need not support full manufacturing/crop lighting,
but outage duration and crop preservation must be part of the load budget.

Carry a 20-year surface-power antimatter bank measured from landing. Antimatter
remains stored energy: local fabrication is assumed capable, but producing new
antimatter still needs net external energy. The colony can manufacture replacement
or expanded energy infrastructure under the adopted premise; the specific
post-20-year primary source is not yet selected. Solar remains the initial backup.

At an assumed 40% electrical conversion efficiency, the ideal 20-year antimatter
fuel masses are about 8.8 g at 1 MW, 26.3 g at 3 MW, 87.8 g at 10 MW and 878 g
at 100 MW average electrical demand, with equal matching matter. These demands
must include plant auxiliaries, containment, fabrication, farming and colony loads.

with spacecraft mass; conversion hardware, shielding, cryogenics and radiators
remain separate mass/power items. These are load cases, not a selected colony
electrical rating or validated conversion efficiency. The calculator now uses

apply. All 1 kg is carried as requested; do not reduce it to the nominal-use amount.

For an explicit heat-rejection comparison, at 10 MWe / 40% conversion source
power is 25 MW and conversion waste heat is 15 MW. If the 10 MW electrical load

do not assume every load needs the same cold loop.

source heat through isolated exchangers to compatible industrial preheating,
drying and water processing, then hot water and occupied-space heating as needed.
Do not assume 600 K conversion waste heat can perform higher-temperature melting
or that a heavily lighted greenhouse needs additional heating. Keep source and
occupied-space fluids isolated. Include thermal stores for demand matching;
they shift heat in time rather than remove it indefinitely.

Consider a bottoming electrical cycle as part of the complete conversion plant,

for it. Retain 40% net electrical efficiency as the study assumption until a
consistent cycle model replaces it. Credit reduced electrical heating demand
only after a heat-source/heat-sink schedule is defined. Final radiators still
reject residual energy: recovery changes useful work and temperature distribution,
not conservation of energy. The 18800 m2 comparison remains an unoptimised
reference, not a fixed radiator requirement or a guaranteed recovery saving.

Reuse suitable cruise cooling and stored electrical energy for commissioning,
then manufacture expanded surface radiator capacity under the local-fabrication
premise. Carry enough starter heat rejection for the initial plant: a factory
cannot build its required cooling before it can run. Surface gravity, coolant
distribution and dust require qualification of reused cruise radiators. Continue
to budget initial power-conversion and cooling hardware as carried mass.

### Inflatable regolith-grown greenhouse

Study 2000 m2 planted/canopy area initially, expandable to 4000 m2. This counts
all stacked layers, not ground footprint. The earlier single-level 2500/5000 m2
gross-floor cases are comparisons. An ideal three-tier arrangement has rack
footprints 667/1333 m2; adding a provisional 25% circulation allowance gives
833/1667 m2 before extra plant, airlocks, soil handling and tall-crop zones.
These are not selected external building dimensions. Divide the initial complex
into eight isolatable bays containing 250 m2 canopy each, arranged in stacked
layers where appropriate, under a large inflatable greenhouse architecture.

NASA controlled-crop studies suggest roughly 50 m2 planted area per adult for
food, depending on crops and conditions. These areas therefore frame first
studies for 36 adults and a growing population; they do not guarantee a complete
diet, successful yields, or capacity for every future generation. Infant needs
are not equivalent to adult crop-area demand. Plan food stores for commissioning
and harvest failure; the first harvest does not occur on arrival.

Use processed local regolith as the mineral substrate, not untreated fertile
soil. Assay chemistry, grain size and contaminants; screen/condition, amend
nutrients and add managed biological inputs. Recycle treated organic wastes and
nutrients. Airless regolith does not contain an assumed terrestrial soil ecology.
Import seed/microbial banks and initial nutrient stocks. Do not assume local
water or fertiliser minerals are accessible before survey and testing.

At illustrative 0.2 m bed depth and 1500 kg/m3 substrate bulk density, 2000 m2
requires 600 t local material. It is not carried interstellar mass, but excavation,
screening, transport and preparation machinery must be carried or constructed.
Vertical stacking does not reduce that substrate inventory. At three tiers,
the average substrate alone is about 900 kg/m2 of rack footprint before plants,
water and structure. Support racks on foundations independently of the inflatable
pressure envelope; use tested local-material structural production where feasible,
not an assumption that a fabric floor or light imported shelf carries it.
That production machinery is separate from both exploration rovers and the
sub-500 kg remote-exploration allowance and must have a declared settlement mass.

Use artificial LED lighting as primary illumination, so the inflatable shell
can prioritise insulation and shielding rather than transparency. Natural light
is optional, not credited in the baseline power budget. Each tier needs light,
irrigation, airflow, sensing, cleaning and access. Select dwarf/short crops for
multi-tier bays; taller crops use fewer tiers. The actual crop mix changes the
effective stacking factor and must meet nutritional, not just leafy-green yield,
requirements. Shelf height must include roots, canopy, lighting and maintenance.

Use controlled CO2 enrichment, with 1000-1200 ppm as an initial reference at
near-Earth total pressure, based on NASA crop-chamber studies. Final control
uses crop needs and CO2 partial pressure if total pressure changes. This is an
oxygen-containing growth atmosphere, not mostly CO2: plants and roots respire.
Provide controlled transfer to living spaces and monitored worker access. Recover
crew respiration CO2 where practical, with storage and treatment to balance crop
cycles. Extra CO2 does not justify assuming food yield increases indefinitely or
that calorie/protein/micronutrient needs are met. Test yield and crop composition.

For electrical sizing only, assume 40 mol/m2/day delivered light, 3 micromol/J
fixture efficacy, 80% fixture-to-canopy delivery, and a 16-hour photoperiod. At
2000 m2 canopy this requires about 0.386 MW daily-average lighting power, or
0.579 MW while all lights are on. At 4000 m2 it doubles. Crop-specific light
requirements, actual fixture performance and optical losses remain to be validated.
Staggering lighting cycles can flatten electrical demand without reducing total
daily energy. Add fans, dehumidification, irrigation, processing and cooling;
lighting electricity ultimately contributes predominantly to settlement heat.
Radiators and antimatter conversion power must be sized alongside the farm.

### Viewer review: spin, landing packing and equipment detail

The two-disc arrangement is an attitude-control choice, not a requirement for
artificial gravity. Opposite equal angular momenta can cancel, although actual
inertias and changing loads need balancing. One disc could house all 18 couples
with revised zoning and an attitude-control solution; no dry-mass saving is yet
quantified. The current model retains both discs pending selection.

Landing now transfers rigid cabin furnishings, bay equipment and pressure doors
into full-scale transport racks, rather than deleting them or shrinking the
complete habitat. Panel bundle envelope volume is conserved. Fabric packing,
folding frames, detachable services, restraints and deployment remain design
assumptions; payload packing does not validate vehicle-wide interference,
structural margins or mass balance. Cabins are unoccupied during descent.
The surface rover is parked clear of both habitat footprints. Machinery detail
shows pumps, filter columns, valve manifolds, fabrication gantries, drawers and
converter fittings. These shapes describe functions and do not establish mature
antimatter, torpor or fully independent fabrication technology.
